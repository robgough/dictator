Two hotkeys

Two hotkeys, two jobs. Dictation drops new text where the cursor is. Assistant edits the text that's already there.

You bind each hotkey in Settings — typically a Fn key for dictation, a different chord for assistant. The menu-bar HUD tells you which one is live while you hold the key, and which pipeline stage it's currently working on.

Dictation

Hold the dictation hotkey, speak, release — the transcript (plus any cleanup passes you've enabled) lands at your cursor. Works in any text field, in any app. The next section walks through the pipeline.

Assistant

A separate hotkey, optionally with text selected. Speak an instruction — Dictator either replaces the selection in place ("tighten this") or drafts something new to the clipboard ("reply with a polite no").

The pipeline

From audio to written text — and the cleanup that makes it readable — all on your Mac.

Most dictation apps either stop at the raw transcript or send it through a paid cloud LLM to clean it up. Dictator runs the whole pipeline locally, and every language-model pass after the transcription has a deterministic safety net: if the model drifts beyond a measurable threshold, that pass reverts.

  1. Transcribe

    WhisperKit or Parakeet (FluidAudio on the Apple Neural Engine) turns your audio into a raw transcript. Both engines run locally; models download once and stay on disk. Pick the one that matches the trade-off you want between accuracy and latency.

  2. Format

    An on-device LLM punctuates, capitalises, and tidies obvious grammar slips. Uses Apple's on-device foundation model when Apple Intelligence is enabled, or a bundled MLX checkpoint as an alternative. Spoken punctuation, line breaks, and named emojis are substituted deterministically before this pass, so they're reliable regardless of which model you pick. Skipped entirely for question-shaped input, where the transcript is already well-punctuated.

    Guard: 60% of input anchor words must survive, and the word count can't grow by more than 15% + 3. Otherwise the raw transcript is used.

  3. Grammar optional

    Fixes contractions, subject-verb agreement, duplicate words — the small things you'd tidy by hand. Off by default; toggle from Settings.

    Guard: word-level edit distance from the input. Reverts if drift exceeds your threshold (15% by default).

  4. Structure optional

    Paragraph breaks and bullet lists for longer dictations — useful when you're talking out an email or a memo, not a one-liner.

    Guard: strict word-sequence equality after lowercasing. Reverts on any word change — bullets and breaks only, never rewording.

A deterministic vocabulary substitution step runs between Format and Grammar — case-insensitive whole-word replacements for names, jargon, and the words your accent always trips up. No model, just your list. The cleanup passes (Format, Grammar, Structure) are the kind of feature most dictation apps charge a subscription for. Here they're included, local, and inspectable.

Dictation modes

Not every dictation needs the full cleanup. Modes let you keep a stripped-back Quick option alongside the full pipeline — and add your own for the contexts you reach for.

A mode bundles which of the optional passes run, the prompts they use, and whether spoken cues and your vocabulary apply. Two come seeded; you can add your own from Settings.

Quick

Every LLM pass off. Raw transcript only — with spoken cues ("comma", "new paragraph", "fire emoji") and your vocabulary substitutions still applied. The fastest path from speech to pasted text. Built-in and locked, so it's always there as the floor.

Write

Every pass on, default prompts — punctuation, grammar, paragraph and bullet structure. The full pipeline. Edit its prompts, or duplicate it as the starting point for a custom mode.

Add your own for the contexts you reach for — an Email mode with a more formal prompt, a Slack mode that keeps things terse, a Code Comments mode that skips structure. Each pass takes either a prompt addendum (appended under the built-in) or a full override, so you can tune one pass without losing the scaffolding on the others.

Cycle the active mode mid-recording with a key (Tab by default) — start in Write, decide partway through it should've been Quick, tap once and the rest of the recording switches. Modes can also auto-activate based on the focused app: bind Email mode to Mail.app, Slack mode to Slack, and Dictator picks the right one as you start to record.

Assistant mode

A second hotkey for editing text by speaking to it.

Hold the assistant hotkey, speak an instruction, release. If you had text selected when you triggered it, Dictator sees both — the selection and the instruction — and the local LLM decides what kind of response you want.

Replace

"Tighten this." "Translate to French." "Fix the comma splice." The selection is replaced in place — and Dictator re-selects the new text so you can iterate ("now make it shorter") without reaching for the mouse.

Draft

"Reply to this with a polite no." "Summarise these notes." "Draft an email about X." The result lands on the clipboard, or in a small floating window you can read and copy from when there's nowhere obvious to paste.

Conversations are multi-turn: follow-ups extend the same thread, with automatic compaction once the model's context window starts filling. Recent conversations are one click away in the menu-bar dropdown.

Choose your models

Two engines for speech-to-text, four small instruction-tuned LLMs for the cleanup passes — or no LLM at all.

All weights are downloaded on first use and stored in ~/Library/Application Support/Dictator/Models/. You can mix and match: smaller transcription model with a bigger LLM, or vice versa.

Language model — used for the cleanup passes and Assistant mode

By default — when Apple Intelligence is enabled — Dictator routes its language-model passes through Apple's on-device foundation model. It's shared with Apple Intelligence (system-resident), so the in-process RAM cost is effectively zero and there's no per-app language-model download. The bundled MLX models below are an alternative if you'd rather pick a specific checkpoint, or aren't using Apple Intelligence; the RAM tiers and table on the rest of this section refer to them.

RAM matters if you pick MLX. Running an MLX language model alongside the transcription model eats a real chunk of memory. The first-run setup wizard detects how much RAM your Mac has and picks defaults that won't push you into swap — but if you override them in Settings, the picker shows a "Fits / Tight / Too large" chip per model so the cost is visible before you commit.

16 GB Minimum

Default is Llama 3.2 1B. Comfortable with the 1B model and the full pipeline running; the 3B works if Dictator is the only memory-hungry app open at the time.

24 GB Recommended

Default is Llama 3.2 3B. Plenty of headroom for the full setup, with room left for everything else you'd usually have open.

32 GB+ Generous

Comfortable with Qwen 2.5 7B for the highest-quality cleanup, and zero swap pressure even with a long Assistant-mode conversation in flight.

Local-first, honestly

Running everything on your Mac is a real trade-off. Worth being clear about both sides.

What you get

  • Privacy. Audio, transcripts, prompts, and conversation history never leave the device. No telemetry, no analytics, no account.
  • Cost. Free after the model downloads. No subscription, no API key, no per-token billing, no surprise bill at the end of the month.
  • Offline. Works on a plane, in a hotel, on a train through a tunnel. Once the models are on disk, the network is irrelevant.
  • Predictable. A vendor can't deprecate the model out from under you or quietly change its behaviour.
  • Inspectable. Open source, so you can see what runs, when, and on what data.

What you give up

  • RAM. With Apple's foundation model handling the LLM passes, any Apple Intelligence–capable Mac is fine. With bundled MLX models, 16 GB minimum for the 1B option; 24 GB+ for the 3B; more for the larger checkpoints.
  • Speed. A small local model takes a couple of seconds per pass. Frontier cloud models like Claude finish in milliseconds — they have orders-of-magnitude more compute behind them.
  • Ceiling. Apple's foundation model and a 3B MLX model are not Claude. For dictation cleanup they're plenty; for genuinely hard text massaging the frontier still wins.
  • First run. The transcription model (~500 MB) downloads on first dictation. If you opt for a bundled MLX language model in place of Apple's, that's another 0.8–2 GB depending on size.
  • Heat. Sustained inference uses the GPU and Apple Neural Engine. Your Mac will get warm under repeated use.

Why I built this

I'm Rob Gough — a tech advisor and fractional CTO, offering a senior pair of eyes on tech strategy and what to build next, drawing on a long career in senior engineering and tech leadership. I'm also building Stay Upfront, a unified support and incident management tool for B2B SaaS companies.

Dictator started as a personal itch. There are genuinely good free dictation tools for the Mac, but the moment I wanted more than the raw transcript — punctuation tidied, "new paragraph" honoured, a sensible bullet list when I rambled — that functionality sat behind a subscription, even when the cleanup ran on a local model. The pieces to do it without one are already open and free: Whisper for the speech-to-text, a small Llama or Qwen for the cleanup, Apple Silicon to run them. Pulling them together turned out to be a fun problem.

I now dictate most of my long-form writing — emails, notes, half this page. I'm a quick typer, but talking is a different kind of fast: ideas land roughly in the order I want them on the page, instead of one keystroke at a time. And the output reads like me — ironically, less AI-generated than my typing does, because my cadence and asides survive the cleanup.

Assistant mode is the second piece of magic — the kind that feels obvious the moment you've used it. Select some text, hold a different hotkey, and ask for the change you want out loud. For a one-word fix, typing is still quicker. But for anything substantive, there's no real comparison. You speak the instruction, the rewrite lands in place, and you're already onto the next thing.

Voice input is going to be a core part of how we use computers — alongside the keyboard and mouse, not replacing them. The big platforms will get there eventually, and when they do it'll feel obvious. Until then, Dictator is a way to live in that future today, on the Mac you already own. I hope you find it useful — and thank you for giving it a try.
